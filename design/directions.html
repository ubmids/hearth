Three ways Hearth could look

Each direction is drawn on the same four real screens: Rose's refused dose on the kitchen Echo Show, Ada's "sort it out" on her phone, the household board, and the Devpost thumbnail. The words are Hearth's own answers from the filmed scenes. Switch the theme above to see each direction at night.

Chosen on 15 October: C, Pharmacy Label, with capitals kept to label headers and stickers and a household label for scenes that are not medicine. The binding spec is design/DESIGN.md. Recommended at the time: A, Care Card. Daylight, a typeface built for low-vision readers, one green for "done" and one red that only ever means "Hearth refused". It is the calmest for a worried family, the most legible on a kitchen counter, and it will not look like any of the dark developer consoles in the Alexa+ gallery. Why, in full.

Direction A Recommended

Care Card

A hospital's calm, without the hospital. Light neutral paper, dark green ink for anything done, and a single red band reserved for a refusal. Every card opens with a status word and a glyph, so nobody has to read colour.

The quoted words a person said are set in a serif italic, so Rose's daughter can see at a glance which line came from Dr. Patel's letter and which from Hearth.

Why it fits

Families in this situation are tired and anxious. A light, ordered screen with big plain words lowers the temperature; the one red band does the alarming. Atkinson was made for exactly the eyes that will read it.

What Phase 2 changes

All four palettes collapse into one. The traffic log becomes an "Under the hood" drawer. Ada's device becomes a portrait phone. Card content is untouched; only the presentation of tone, type and spacing changes.

Direction B

Lamplight

The kitchen in the evening. A warm dark screen like a bedside lamp, a serif that reads like a handwritten family note, and one amber for "this happened". The fact that matters most, the time Grace gave the dose, is set as large as a clock.

This is the closest to what Hearth has now, grown up: the warmth stays, the developer chrome and the cyan go.

Why it fits

It is the most emotional of the three: a dark warm screen in a kitchen at dusk is where this story happens, and the serif makes Hearth sound like a person who cares rather than a system.

What Phase 2 changes

The least: the console and card keep their dark warm base, the sim loses its navy and cyan, the serif arrives. Cheapest to apply, but the least different from what judges will see elsewhere.

Direction C

Pharmacy Label

Every family already trusts one piece of design about medicine: the label on the bottle, with its black rules and its coloured warning stickers. Hearth's cards become labels. The red sticker says what not to do, the yellow sticker carries the rule and who set it.

It is the most distinctive of the three and says "serious about safety" before a word is read.

Why it fits

A medicine label is a design every carer already reads under stress. It makes the refusal feel like a real safety mechanism and would stand out instantly in a gallery of dark consoles.

What Phase 2 changes

The most: new composition for every card, a label grammar for non-medicine scenes (the handover, the order) that does not yet exist, and all-caps headings that cut against the older-reader principle.

Compare and pick

Recommendation: A, Care Card

The Design criterion asks whether the product is "a complete, coherent product experience" and whether the interaction is "well-considered for the target device". A answers both most directly: one system across every surface, set at the size a kitchen and an 84-year-old need. B is the warmer mood but the most ordinary in the field. C is the boldest idea but fights the audience's eyes and only fits the medicine scenes.
